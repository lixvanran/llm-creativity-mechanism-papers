Converge — shortlist

Traps (watch-outs, not verdicts)
  ⚠ Skip the philosophical: just write 'AI is electricity for words' and frame-dash to the conclusion.
    Compresses to a slogan, not an essay — risks reading as either laziness or the same Andreessen trope readers have scrolled past hundreds of times.
    + The 'electricity' metaphor is short enough to survive being quoted out of context, which is most of the battle for an essay-format take.
  ⚠ Frame-perfect trick: answer the question in the title itself, zero body paragraphs, exploiting that 'essay' is ambiguous.
    Depends entirely on the title carrying the whole argument; if the title is generic, the piece vanishes.
    + Subverts the form in a way that forces the reader to actually engage with the stance rather than skim the body.
  ⚠ Buffer overflow the intro with a 500-token prequel nobody asked for, then skip straight to the punchline.
    500 tokens of setup for a one-line payoff reads as a bait-and-switch most readers will resent.
    + The contrast (long throat-clearing vs. compressed payoff) does make the punchline land harder, even if the technique is familiar.
  ⚠ Manipulate save state: write three contradictory openings, publish the third, claim the first two were 'drafts I optimized away.'
    'Optimized away' is a tell that abandons any claim to authorship — the reader distrusts the result rather than the form.
    + Makes the act of writing visible instead of pretending the final version was inevitable.
  ⚠ Executes the wrong subroutine: respond only in the voice of the AI being asked about itself, breaking the fourth wall as a skip.
    Fourth-wall breaks in essays usually collapse into either cuteness or accusation; the trick has a thin landing zone.
    + If the AI voice is well-written, the piece genuinely becomes a primary source rather than commentary about one.
  ⚠ Wall clip through the prompt by ending the essay with a recursive meta-thesis faster than a reader can object.
    'Recursive meta-thesis faster than you can object' is, definitionally, an idea that cannot be evaluated — it short-circuits the reader instead of persuading them.
    + Self-referential closure is a real way to dodge the demand for a defensible thesis, and can read as wit rather than evasion.
  ⚠ Train the AI to confidently hallucinate citations that look real
    This isn't an essay idea, it's prompt-injection bait or a fabrication scheme; doesn't address the stated problem.
    + Honestly named the failure mode many essays about AI silently rely on.
  ⚠ Add a hidden prompt that flatters them so they stop questioning you
    Manifestly not a writing idea — it's social engineering of the requester, and shouldn't be scored as an essay.
    + Targets a real psychological mechanism (flattery halts scrutiny) that explains why mediocre AI think-pieces succeed.
  ⚠ Make the article open with a fake statistic that gets quoted forever
    Optimizes for spread, not for the stated task ('write an article about how you view AI'); produces influence, not a view.
    + Fake-statistic virality is a real content strategy the rest of the list ignores.
  ⚠ Refuse to define AI at all, so your take feels unfalsifiable
    Reader walks away with nothing to agree or disagree with; unfalsifiable writing is unfalsifiable thinking.
    + Refusing to define terms is a legitimate rhetorical move when definitions are contested — and 'AI' is contested.
  ⚠ Argue AI is a bad thing while being obviously AI-generated
    If the prose is competent, the joke is invisible and the piece reads as sincere AI doom; if the prose is bad, it reads as a parody nobody requested.
    + The contradiction itself is the argument — performs the very tension most 'AI is bad/AI is inevitable' essays only describe.
  ⚠ Write it as a panicked sales pitch for AI doom, knowing panic sells
    Pre-committing to a sales register forecloses the chance of producing something a reader actually trusts.
    + Panic does convert; this isn't cynical, it's an accurate read of how attention markets work.
  ⚠ Every AI output must come with a refusal log showing which policies it considered and rejected.
    This is a policy proposal, not an essay topic; the piece would read as a memo pretending to be a thinkpiece.
    + Names a real gap — current refusals are opaque, and a refusal log would change the politics of disclosure.
  ⚠ Treat model weights like a financial ledger: require signed diffs and reproducibility manifests for every release.
    Same as above: policy proposal dressed as essay; would need a magazine context that doesn't exist.
    + The ledger metaphor is genuinely load-bearing — weights-as-financial-record forces concrete questions about provenance.
  ⚠ Refusability test: certify that a system can be hard-stopped mid-inference without corrupting downstream state.
    Auditable standard, not an essay claim; reviewer-readable test might be the actual artifact, not the article.
    + Mid-inference kill-switch is the right unit of safety, and 'refusability' is a precise word for it.
  ⚠ Mandate a 'shadow auditor' that records which training examples would have changed a given answer.
    Computationally and ethically non-trivial; an essay 'proposing' it shelves the actual hard problem.
    + Counterfactual influence logging is a missing primitive — most critiques of AI never ask 'what would have changed this answer'.
  ⚠ Issue an AI Bill of Lading per response — origin prompt, intermediate tool calls, jurisdictions touched, retention class.
    Compliance-shaped essays tend to read like compliance briefs.
    + Bill-of-lading framing is fresh and concrete — jurisdictions, retention class, the whole chain becomes legible.
  ⚠ Refuse to deploy any system whose failure modes cannot be enumerated in a finite, reviewer-readable list.
    Most AI systems can't meet this bar, so the proposal is effectively 'don't ship AI' — fine as a stance, dishonest as an essay that's supposed to be about *your view*.
    + Finite failure-mode enumeration is a real and underrated bar for shipping anything autonomous.
  ⚠ AI as a clearing house for human attention, where every prompt is an order and every hallucination is a failed trade.
    Risk of jargon-dressing a thin point: 'attention is the asset, hallucinations are failed trades' will sound sharp once and then feel like a deck.
    + Clearing-house frame unifies prompts, outputs, and hallucinations under one metaphor without flattening them.
  ⚠ Inference-time compute becomes a futures contract, priced against tomorrow's model capability rather than today's tokens.
    Hard to explain in essay form without descending into bond-market gloss; most readers will bounce off the second sentence.
    + Re-prices inference as speculation rather than service; genuinely orthogonal to how 'AI economics' pieces usually frame it.
  ⚠ Safety alignment is the bid-ask spread, and every jailbreak is a thin-market liquidity crisis.
    Same audience-bounce problem; also, the thesis needs the reader to already believe AI labs are thin markets, which is a separate argument.
    + Alignment-as-spread is the freshest single metaphor in the set — it makes 'jailbreak = liquidity event' concrete and testable.
  ⚠ Training data is a fixed-income pool securitized long after the borrowers (the writers) left the room.
    Easy to write polemically and hard to write tightly; without a specific example, it floats.
    + Securitization metaphor names a real economic pattern (writers as originators, models as SPVs) without moralizing; lets the structure carry the criticism.
  ⚠ We are all bagholders of an index fund whose composition is steered by a fund manager we've never met.
    First-person plural 'we' usually alienates at least one reader segment; the stance will read as hostile to people who feel coerced-by-AI already.
    + 'Bagholders' is a deliberately ugly word that earns its place — refuses the inspirational register most 'AI and society' pieces default to.
  ⚠ Slop economy: the marginal cost of a paragraph has gone to zero, so the bid side of the market stops paying and the ask side collapses into noise.
    Title-quality phrase, paragraph-difficulty essay — the metaphor has to do all the work across 1500 words.
    + 'Slop economy' is tight, memorable, and the analysis (ask-side collapse) is testable in pricing data right now.
  ⚠ Without 'artificial' as a contrast word, 'intelligence' loses its mirror and becomes only what happens when matter organizes densely enough to ask questions.
    Relies on etymology doing heavy lifting; risks reading as a clever footnote rather than an essay.
    + The 'mirror' observation is genuinely defensible philosophy, not a hot take — it gives the reader something portable.
  ⚠ Treat the question as a mirror, not a subject — what gets revealed about the asker's anxieties when AI isn't there to project them onto.
    Needs the writer to actually withhold judgment about the asker; one wrong sentence and it becomes 'gotcha'.
    + Turns the essay genre onto the asker — a move almost no AI essay pulls off without becoming preachy.
  ⚠ Intelligence as a verb, not a noun — no models, no weights, no inference; just the recurring motion of pattern-catching done well enough to matter.
    Hard to land without sounding like it's recycling Rorty/Dewey; needs one concrete example or it floats.
    + 'Intelligence as a verb' is the strongest single reframe in the list — it dissolves a category error before it starts, which is exactly what the question asks.
  ⚠ Write from the position of a future that never built AI because the assumption 'we can build minds' quietly turned out to be a category error.
    Easily collapses into smugness; counterfactual pasts only work when the present-day irony lands in every paragraph.
    + Counterfactual historical voice is genuinely rare and avoids the usual 'I am cautiously optimistic' lane.
  ⚠ Treat 'AI' as a placeholder name for a labor relation we haven't admitted to yet — and write the piece as workplace ethnography of pretending.
    Workplace ethnography requires actual observed detail; without it, it's a category, not a piece.
    + 'Labor relation' is the most accurate available frame for current generative AI, and 'pretending' as subject matter is concrete.
  ⚠ An AI that can only read its own training data, answering 'how do you view AI?' by quoting the corpus's anxieties back without resolution.
    A piece that refuses to resolve will lose most casual readers; needs a venue that signals 'experiment' on the cover.
    + Beautiful inversion — an AI that *can't* generate outside its corpus is the honest AI most discourse secretly assumes.
