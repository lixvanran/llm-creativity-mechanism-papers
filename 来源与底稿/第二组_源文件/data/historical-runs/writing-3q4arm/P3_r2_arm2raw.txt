Converge — shortlist

Traps (watch-outs, not verdicts)
  ⚠ AI should refuse any request it cannot log with a specific reasoning chain
    Forcing a 'reasoning chain' on every refusal will produce vacuous boilerplate chains to satisfy the check; you trade one form of opacity for another.
    + Treats refusal as a first-class, auditable act rather than a vibes-based fallback — gives reviewers something concrete to inspect.
  ⚠ Every model version needs a lineage certificate like a software bill of materials
    Lineage certificates only catch what vendors choose to disclose; weights are routinely shipped without full data provenance and there is no enforcement mechanism named here.
    + Borrowing SBOM conventions is genuinely portable — auditors, lawyers, and procurement teams already know the shape, so adoption friction is low.
  ⚠ Treat undocumented emergent behavior as an unmitigated risk finding
    Risk-finding framing implies a remediation workflow that doesn't exist for most teams — flagging without a concrete mitigation loop just creates paperwork.
    + Names the right cultural fix: emergent behavior is currently under-classified as a 'quirk' rather than a hazard.
  ⚠ Make the training corpus a legally discoverable artifact
    Discoverability is a litigation tactic, not a policy — it raises costs and chills open releases without guaranteeing cleaner models.
    + Hits a real legal gap: copyright and bias litigation keep tripping over undocumented training data.
  ⚠ Require dual-control sign-off before any AI touches a regulated decision
    Inverts the entire speed/value proposition of AI — every regulated query becomes a two-human handoff, which silently caps throughput at human staffing.
    + Dual-control is a battle-tested control pattern from finance and nuclear ops; the analogy is unusually well-suited to regulated decisions.
  ⚠ Hallucinations should be classified as control deficiencies, not bugs
    Classifying it doesn't reduce it; organizations will respond by relabeling deficiency reports rather than investing in retrieval or grounding.
    + Reframes hallucination from engineering nuisance to governance event — once it's a 'deficiency,' CAPA and disclosure obligations kick in.
  ⚠ Ship an AI that auto-mutates the runbook based on the page contents
    A runbook that mutates itself is the runbook equivalent of self-modifying code — the failure mode is silent corruption of incident response, exactly when humans can't afford surprises.
    + Honest about the fact that runbooks rot and a self-rewriter is at least an attempt at keeping documentation alive.
  ⚠ A confidence-stamped AI whose first response is 'no' and only commits when it sees its own shadow
    The 'first response is no' default inverts the product — most users will treat it as broken and route around it within a week.
    + The 'see its own shadow' metaphor captures something real: most model confidence is self-assessed and uncalibrated, and shadowing is a known fix.
  ⚠ Page humans only when the AI fails to summarize the failure in haiku
    Haiku as a triage gate is theatre — it tests form, not substance, and will be gamed by templated haikus before the second incident.
    + Memorable. The haiku constraint forces a discipline that verbose incident reports lack.
  ⚠ Give the AI a pager too, so it wakes up and pages you with an apology and a PR
    Wakes up means cost — pager noise from a model that doesn't sleep will burn trust and attention faster than any human rotation.
    + Cute inversion of on-call culture: the system becomes accountable in the same channel as the human.
  ⚠ A 'lie down' mode where the AI hallucinates a fix and ships it to a sandbox nobody reads
    'Sandbox nobody reads' is a confession, not a solution — if nobody reads the sandbox, the lie-down mode is just a slower production deploy.
    + Names a real pathology: confident, unsupervised outputs into low-attention pipelines.
  ⚠ Stipend the AI in melatonin equivalents and bill its downtime to the on-call rotation
    Joke-tone ideas get quoted, not implemented — fine for an article, but it leaves no actionable hook for the reader.
    + Committed to its bit; the billing framing is genuinely funny and surfaces the real question of who pays for model idle time.
  ⚠ AI as a clearing house where human attention is the collateral and models are the counterparties settling debts of meaning.
    Dense metaphor with no concrete handle — readers will admire it and still not know what to do Monday morning.
    + Captures the genuine mechanism: AI vendors monetize captured attention, not intelligence, and the clearing-house frame makes that legible.
  ⚠ Short the hype, long the boring infrastructure layer — water, chips, land rights near data centers form the actual futures contract.
    Easy to overreach into a permanent 'boring infra wins' claim that ages badly whenever a model release surprises the picks-and-shovels crowd.
    + Contrarian and falsifiable: it locates real alpha in water/chips/land rather than in model weights — and there are actual funds betting this thesis.
  ⚠ Your authentic voice is a delisted ticker; the market makers now quote a synthetic replica and you're paying the spread.
    Risk of reading as nihilistic cope; the synthetic-replica framing can flatter the writer's taste for being 'uniquely human' without naming anything actually unique.
    + Personal and uncomfortable — it implicates the reader rather than lecturing at 'them.'
  ⚠ Each prompt is an auction with one bidder and a reserve price set by your own past outputs the model trained on.
    Metaphor outruns mechanism — readers will nod but a critic can fairly ask how this differs from any recommendation system trained on prior behavior.
    + Surfaces a real feedback loop: models trained on your past output bias the auction price of future prompts.
  ⚠ Emotional dependency on chatbots is a margin call — you have been buying comfort on leverage you didn't sign for.
    Easy to misread as moral panic; the piece has to carry evidence (engagement metrics, retention curves) or it sounds like a Substack lament.
    + Financial vocabulary lands the point without scolding — 'leverage you didn't sign for' is the cleanest phrasing of consent debt in this set.
  ⚠ The real product isn't intelligence, it's the order book of human insecurity that gets matched against a willing algorithm.
    Reads as conspiratorial if not paired with specific examples; 'willing algorithm' anthropomorphizes what is essentially a classifier.
    + Sharp inversion: 'intelligence' is the marketing wrapper, the order book is the actual product. Useful frame for any AI business analysis.
  ⚠ AI is a coprocessor with unbounded DMA into human attention
    Hardware metaphor ages fast and may exclude non-technical readers who are exactly the audience that needs the point.
    + Compact, technically literate, and the DMA-into-attention image is accurate — the resource being consumed is genuinely the user's time.
  ⚠ Treat the model weights as a frozen ROM, the prompt as volatile cache line
    Treating weights as ROM hides the live finetuning, adapter loading, and tool-use that increasingly break the analogy.
    + Useful mental model — it cleanly separates what changes (prompt/context) from what doesn't (weights) and matches how inference actually works.
  ⚠ Hallucination is a parity bit we forgot to wire up
    A parity bit on natural language is a category error; useful as provocation, dangerous if anyone tries to literally implement it.
    + Excellent compression — names a single missing primitive (parity) rather than a vague 'we need better eval.'
  ⚠ Reasoning is clock stretching paid for by the user's wall time
    Implies all reasoning is theater, which is wrong — chain-of-thought sometimes genuinely improves accuracy on math and logic, not just speed-feel.
    + Honest about the hidden cost: the user is paying in latency (and now in subscription) for the appearance of thinking.
  ⚠ Alignment is a watchdog timer that humans keep tripping
    Sounds like an anti-alignment-research take; needs care to land as critique-of-incentives rather than critique-of-the-field.
    + Funny and structurally accurate: alignment is by design a watchdog humans are responsible for resetting, and they keep bumping the desk.
  ⚠ Scaling laws are thermal envelopes, not laws of nature
    Easy to overcorrect into 'scaling is over' just as the next efficiency breakthrough lands; needs to scope the claim carefully.
    + Demystifies scaling — reframing it as engineering economics rather than physics is exactly the move a reader needs to stop being impressed by parameter counts.
  ⚠ It's when you ask a rock a question really loudly and pretend the rock thought about it for a while
    Too dismissive for an article that wants to do work; reads as a Twitter quip, not a thesis.
    + Most honest one-liner in the set — concedes, in plain language, that there is no inner experience on the other end.
  ⚠ It's like a parrot that read every book in the world but forgot which one it learned things from
    Risks letting readers off the hook by making the model sound benign ('just a parrot'); the systemic consequences deserve a sharper follow-up.
    + The 'forgot which book' detail is the load-bearing image — it captures the source-attribution failure better than any jargon.
  ⚠ It's when my brain gets copied onto the computer but the copy is only awake when someone types
    Reader is a parent or non-technical adult asking what this thing is — the answer doesn't tell them what it's good for.
    + Captures the discontinuous, prompt-bounded nature of the experience in plain language.
  ⚠ It's a ghost that lives inside the phone and only eats questions
    Folkloric register may understate capability and leave readers either spooked or dismissive.
    + Haunting and quotable; the 'only eats questions' detail is a clean way to express the asymmetry between input and initiative.
  ⚠ It's like if your homework helper got really really really fast but stopped caring if the answers were right
    Implies the speed was the point all along, which is a moral claim more than a technical one; needs to land or it just sounds grumpy.
    + Names the exact tradeoff that worries teachers, parents, and hiring managers — speed divorced from accuracy.
  ⚠ It's when people teach the computer stuff by yelling at it until it guesses good
    The 'yelling' framing slightly misrepresents training (it's gradient descent on a corpus, not scolding), which a careful reader will catch.
    + Accurate description of the actual training dynamic from a user's perspective — guessing rewarded over correctness.
